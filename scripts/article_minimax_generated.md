# Week of March 30: Children's Fingerprints Found on 15,000-Year-Old Clay Beads, Oldest Dog DNA, and More

*Researchers have identified children's fingerprints on 15,000-year-old clay beads from Natufian sites in Israel, showing that children participated alongside adults in symbolic object production using red ochre coloring techniques that predate agriculture. Ancient DNA analysis has pushed back the earliest genetic evidence of domestic dogs to approximately 15,800 years ago at Pinabashi, Turkey, while an 18-million-year-old ape fossil named Masripithecus moghraensis discovered in Egypt's Wadi Moghra region challenges the long-held view that East Africa was the cradle of modern ape evolution. Additionally, a 12th-century sword embedded in stone at the Chapel of Montesiepi in Tuscany has been verified as genuine through material analysis.*

---

## New Excavations & Fieldwork

Dor Lauron and colleagues at Hebrew University of Jerusalem have identified children's fingerprints on approximately 15,000-year-old clay beads recovered from Natufian sites in Israel, including Nahal Oren[4][9][10][2]. Their research, published in Science Advances, examined 142 clay ornaments found across four sites—Elwad, Nahal Oren, Hayanim, and Anan Malaha—revealing that both children and adults participated in crafting these objects, demonstrating collaborative symbolic production among hunter-gatherers[6][5][7][3][1]. The beads were shaped into cylinders, discs, and ellipses, with these forms remaining consistent across three millennia of production[8].

![Children's fingerprints identified on 15,000-year-old clay beads in Israel](/api/news/screenshots/_5_bp8maWa8_3316.webp)

The findings show that symbolic expression and social learning through craft predate the emergence of agriculture by thousands of years [7][3][1][2]. Notably, researchers identified 19 bead shapes resembling foods that Natufian hunter-gatherers collected—including barley, lentils, peas, and flax—plants that would later become foundational to Neolithic agriculture[1]. The beads also represent the earliest known use of red ochre coloring technique anywhere in the world. This evidence suggests children played meaningful roles in cultural transmission and technological innovation long before sedentary agricultural societies emerged.

The sword embedded in stone at the Chapel of Montesiepi in Tuscany has been verified as a genuine 12th-century artifact through comprehensive material analysis, ruling out any possibility of modern forgery[12][13][11]. Chemist Luigi Garlaschelli of the University of Pavia drilled directly into the rock surrounding the blade and confirmed that both the exposed portion and a hidden section beneath the stone are continuous, constituting a single weapon[11]. Ground penetrating radar investigation revealed an unexpected cavity approximately 2 meters by 1 meter beneath the stone, potentially indicating an underlying grave. The historical context adds another layer of mystery: the first written account of the Arthurian "sword in the stone" legend appears around 1200 CE, occurring decades after the reported death of Galgano Giati in 1181. Scholars have noted linguistic proximity between Galgano Giati's name and a Knight of the Round Table, prompting speculation that the legend may have traveled from Italy to England.

## Remote Sensing & Technology

Research by Mark Norden proposes that the Great Sphinx and Second Pyramid together form an "horizon" (arket/arkhet) symbol specifically aligned to summer solstice sunset. Norden's findings also suggest a proposed second Sphinx site at a 296° bearing would align to summer solstice sunset circa 6,500 BCE, with the opposite bearing at 116° aligning to winter solstice sunrise during that same period. These alignments, if verified, would indicate remarkably precise astronomical knowledge during Egypt's predynastic era.

![Baltray standing stones discovered with winter solstice alignment to Rockabill Island in 1999](/api/news/screenshots/NVQINlsyWvI_216.webp)

Beyond these solstice alignments, Andrew Collins has proposed a Giza grid measurement system based on units called "reads," each comprising 10.5 feet. According to this system, the distance between the existing Sphinx and the proposed second Sphinx site measures exactly 378 reads, and the grid extends from Jebel Gibli in the southeast to the Tomb of the Birds in the northwest. Supporting these claims, a Japanese research team operating between 2021 and 2023 employed ground penetration radar to detect a previously unknown L-shaped underground structure situated 2–3 meters below the surface near the Tomb of Henu. These discoveries, while intriguing, originate from a YouTube video and remain to be independently verified through peer-reviewed research.

![Geometric grid system and solstice alignments at Giza Plateau](/api/news/screenshots/SKJsolWOAqc_2765.webp)

Claims of remarkable precision in Wisconsin's ancient effigy mound layouts have emerged from a recent survey, with some geometries allegedly accurate to within a single minute of arc—comparable to the accuracy of Egyptian pyramids. A YouTube video from the Institute for Natural Philosophy, an informal community exploring philosophical questions about reality and human nature, presented these findings as a breakthrough in understanding the builders' technical capabilities. However, the precision claims originate from only this single source, and previous mound surveys had been conducted with compass control accurate to only a couple of degrees, suggesting the actual precision is lower than these claims indicate. Geometry patterns have been discovered in effigy mound layouts that can be "read like a new book," though this interpretive framework comes from the same YouTube origin.

![Wisconsin effigy mound survey reveals minute-accurate ancient layout precision](/api/news/screenshots/VIhbcSYLnqk_319.webp)

The effigy mound tradition was built by nearly two dozen communities across the region, with many mounds located where major cities stand today [15]. A Luther College professor named Betts began surveying a national monument in 2022 using remote sensing technology to study hidden complexities in the mounds [14]. Unlike the precision claims circulating in online discussions, Betts's work represents a more traditional archaeological approach to understanding these ancient structures.

## Bioarchaeology & Ancient DNA

Ancient DNA analysis has identified dog remains at the Pinabashi site in Turkey dating to approximately 15,800 years ago, representing the oldest genetic evidence of domestic dogs known to date [19] [22] [16] [18]. This discovery pushes back the earliest direct genetic evidence of dogs by approximately 5,000 years compared to the previous record of 10,900 years ago from Karelia, Russia [24] [16] [25] [26] [20]. Genetic separation between dogs and wolves occurred at least 16,000 years ago during early domestication [21], and the studies confirm dogs existed during the Paleolithic period, before the development of agriculture [23] [25] [26].

![Ancient dog DNA reveals domestication 15,800 years ago in Anatolia](/api/news/screenshots/_5_bp8maWa8_509.webp)

The spread of dogs across Western Eurasia was remarkably rapid—by 14,300 years ago, they had appeared at sites separated by over 4,000 kilometers, including Pinabashi in Turkey and Goff's Cave in the UK. Isotopic chemistry from Pinabashi reveals these dogs were fed a fish-rich diet matching their human owners [24], while intentional burials sometimes placed alongside humans suggest dogs held symbolic significance within these hunter-gatherer communities [19]. As Professor Gregor Larsson from Oxford noted, dogs spread quickly across Europe as hunting partners, making them the first domesticated animal, predating livestock by thousands of years [17].

![Rock-cut graves across Europe span 20th century BC to 11th century AD](/api/news/screenshots/zLvv-XancwU_415.webp)

An 18-million-year-old ape fossil discovered in Egypt's Wadi Moghra region is forcing scientists to reconsider where our earliest ancestors came from. Masripithecus moghraensis, an Early Miocene ape represented by a remarkably preserved jawbone, has been shown through phylogenetic analysis to sit closer to the root of crown Hominoidea—the lineage leading to all living apes—than any previously known Early Miocene species. The fossil's robust anatomy, large canines, and molars with rounded, heavily textured chewing surfaces suggest a flexible fruit-based diet. This discovery challenges the long-held view that East Africa was the cradle of modern ape evolution, instead pointing toward a northern Afro-Arabian origin for crown Hominoidea [27] [30] [29]. During the Early Miocene, Wadi Moghra was a lush forest corridor with river systems and estuaries, a stark contrast to the desert environment present today. The research employed tip-dating methodology that integrates fossil age directly into evolutionary tree analysis, and the findings appeared in Science [28]. This specimen represents the first ape fossil found in Egypt, and researchers suggest it may be the common ancestor from which all living apes descend, fundamentally reshaping the evolutionary story of our closest relatives.

## In Brief

For decades, early ape evolution research has been anchored in East Africa, where the richest fossil record on the continent has been excavated at sites in Kenya and Uganda [33]. Researchers argue this geographic focus may have shaped scientific opinion about where hominid origins occurred, with sample bias potentially compounding over time as more digs in the region yielded more discoveries. Northern Africa functioned as a biogeographic crossroads connecting Africa and Eurasia during the Early Miocene, a role that has only recently gained attention.

![Early ape evolution research biased toward East Africa due to fossil sampling](/api/news/screenshots/rMap2vR6_Qc_302.webp)

A newly discovered fossil ape from Egypt named *Masripithecus moghraensis* is challenging the long-held view that East Africa was the cradle of modern ape ancestors. Dating to approximately 17–18 million years ago, the specimen was found in the Wadi Moghra region of northern Egypt [32][34][31][35][33]. The discovery suggests that early apes may have radiated from this region rather than from East Africa, predating major ape expansions into Eurasia around 15 million years ago. Some researchers caution that a single find does not yet provide enough evidence to definitively rewrite ape evolutionary history [36], and it remains unclear whether *Masripithecus* represents an ancestral form to all modern apes or a regional lineage that contributed to later dispersals [32][35]. However, the finding has prompted broader questions about sampling bias in paleoanthropology and the need to expand exploration beyond traditional East African sites.

![Vulcansky Dolmen: unique monolithic chamber carved from single sandstone block in Caucasus](/api/news/screenshots/NdzrleZlOK0_621.webp)

Archaeological research has confirmed that Cahokia Woodhenge functioned as both a solar and lunar calendar, tracking celestial alignments with remarkable precision. The solstice angle aligns from the center of the wooden post rings at the site, and researchers have documented that maximum and minimum lunar declination angles were built into the configuration alongside solstice angles. Professor Al Fang at Illinois State University independently confirmed these astronomical alignments, verifying that Mound 72 is positioned at the lunar declination angle from Woodhenge, effectively serving as a lunar mound. Modern archaeological work using LiDAR imagery and landscape analysis has documented solar, lunar, and Milky Way alignments at Cahokia, with these alignments intersecting prominent bluffs used as horizon markers [44].

![Cahokia Woodhenge: Solstice and lunar alignments match European Stonehenge](/api/news/screenshots/ebAxua9aQI4_1614.webp)

Cahokia Woodhenge actually consisted of five separate timber circles—Woodhenge I through V—each with a different size and number of posts [39] [41]. The reconstructed version measured approximately 240 feet across with 24 wooden posts evenly spaced around a central post [38] [43]. The structure was positioned about 850 meters (half a mile) west of Monks Mound and aligned with the equinox, while simultaneously tracking solstice angles for agricultural timing, including planting and harvest periods [40] [37] [45] [41] [42] [43]. While Woodhenge was constructed from timber rather than stone, its sophisticated astronomical design—combining solar and lunar alignments in a monumental earthwork landscape—demonstrates a comparable intellectual achievement to European megalithic monuments.

At the Round Rock Memorial Park and Bull Creek sites in Texas, cart ruts carved into limestone creek beds extend underwater for at least 100 yards, representing one of the most significant features of these embedded trackways. The ruts are not merely surface features but are geologically embedded in the limestone bedrock itself, with no raised material alongside the grooves, indicating sustained wear into solid rock rather than displacement of soil. Remarkably, these deep-embedded ruts survived heavy equipment scraping during 2024 park renovations, demonstrating the extent to which the tracks have become part of the limestone substrate. At the Round Rock Memorial Park site, the ruts measure 2-3 inches in depth, with some exceeding one foot. The Bull Creek location features multiple overlapping cart tracks, including 4-wheel turning patterns that reveal how vehicles navigated the area. The Bull Creek wagon ruts are located at one of the many Bull Creek crossings of the road that eventually became Lakewood Drive in Northwest Austin [47]. These ruts provide a window into Austin area life in the 1800s [48] [46]. Cart ruts are formally defined as parallel recesses in rock formations created by wheeled vehicles, with academic characterization studies comparing Texas sites to those in the Azores [49].

![Texas cart ruts embedded in limestone creek beds extend underwater](/api/news/screenshots/ipwhZGPxXso_1592.webp)

In 1999, researchers Richard Moore and Michael Burn made a striking discovery at Baltray, a coastal village at the mouth of the River Boyne in County Louth, Ireland [50]. The site contains two standing stones, the larger of which stands less than three meters in height, and the researchers confirmed that the larger stone aligns with Rockabill Island for the winter solstice sunrise in December 1999 [51] [52]. This orientation mirrors the famous winter solstice sunrise alignment of Newgrange in the Boyne Valley. Baltray corresponds to Inbher Colptha, described in ancient mythology as the mouth of the River Boyne. Folklore connects this place to Balor, a sun deity, and a magic cow, with folkloric interpretation suggesting the moon as the cow and Venus as the calf. The discovery was first documented in a MegalithomaniaUK recording from 2007.

The discovery of a child's skeleton dating to approximately 29,000 years ago in Thailand represents the oldest human remains found in the country [54] [53]. The remains, nicknamed 'Pangpond,' were unearthed at a cave site and offer unprecedented insight into Thailand's prehistoric inhabitants during the Middle Ice Age. This groundbreaking find significantly pushes back the timeline of known human occupation in the region, as prior to this discovery, older and more complete specimens of this age had not been identified within Thailand's borders.

The excavation site is located in Khao Sam Roi Yot National Park in Prachuap Khiri Khan province, Thailand [53]. The area is part of a broader region known for its extensive cave systems, where numerous ancient human remains have been discovered over the years. Researchers continue to analyze the skeletal remains and their archaeological context to learn more about the physical characteristics, diet, and lifestyle of Thailand's earliest inhabitants during this ancient period.

The absence of human remains at Akrotiri represents the most striking aspect of this Bronze Age site. Despite extensive excavations, not a single human skeletal remains has ever been found at Akrotiri [57] [58]. This absence is particularly remarkable given that the Thera eruption was cataclysmic in scale—one of the largest volcanic events in Earth's history [57]. Archaeological evidence suggests residents packed their belongings, evacuated the elderly, children, pets, and farm animals by ship, departing as refugees before the disaster unfolded. Major earthquakes preceded the volcanic eruption in around 1550 BC, likely providing the warning residents needed to escape [55].

![Akrotiri residents successfully evacuated before massive volcanic eruption around 1550 BC](/api/news/screenshots/3M3jL27iIPM_1503.webp)

However, the narrative of complete evacuation faces a complication: one victim of the Thera eruption has been found—but not at Akrotiri itself, raising questions about whether the evacuation was truly universal [57]. The dating of the eruption itself remains disputed, with one source citing 1627 BC while others place it around 1550 BC [56].

Rock-cut graves across Europe represent one of the continent's most enduring funerary traditions, spanning approximately 3,100 years from at least the 20th century BC to the 11th century AD [59]. The Iberian Peninsula contains the biggest and most impressive collection of these carved burial sites in Europe, and in central Portugal, rock-cut graves are the most abundant funerary remains left by early medieval communities. Sicily demonstrates remarkable continuity in this practice—Cava Dispica contains rock-cut graves dating as early as the 20th century BC, while the Pantalica necropolis preserves ancient rock-cut tombs representing Bronze Age Sicilian burial practices [60]. The Heysham site in northern England contains six rock-cut graves carved directly into millstone grit bedrock, with five featuring socket features at the head end [59].

This burial tradition continued into historic periods—Monte Sarraceno in Italy belongs to the Daunian period of the 8th century BC, and St Odile in France contains early medieval rock-cut graves from approximately the 7th century AD. The practice extended well beyond European borders, with Punic or Carthaginian rock-cut graves documented near Tangier, Morocco, and the tradition stretching from Thracia and Macedonia through to Armenia, suggesting a remarkably broad geographic distribution.
